\chapter{Architecture and RTL Design}
\label{ch:architecture-rtl}

This chapter presents the implemented controller, the flagship contribution of the thesis. It is
organised in two parts. \emph{Part~A} builds a system-level mental model: the three-layer
architecture, the top-level module hierarchy, the end-to-end transaction dataflow, the clock and
reset conventions, the design boundary derived qualitatively from the CHIPS Alliance
\texttt{i3c-core} reference~\cite{chipsalliance_i3c}, the queue and descriptor formats, and the
error-handling model. \emph{Part~B} then walks through the register-transfer-level (RTL) design of
each subsystem in turn, culminating in the central \FlowStateCount-state \texttt{flow_active} command
finite-state machine (FSM). Throughout, the protocol facts follow the MIPI I3C Basic
v1.1.1 specification~\cite{mipi_i3c_basic}; quantitative size and result metrics are deferred to
\cref{ch:results} so that this chapter stays focused on structure and behaviour. To keep the chapter
within budget, the largest reference tables --- the complete register map, the full descriptor
bit-field layouts, the full per-module port lists, and the exhaustive state-transition tables --- are
relocated to the appendices and cited inline with a representative excerpt.

\section{Three-layer architecture}
\label{sec:arch-three-layer}

The controller is organised as three concentric layers, each with a single responsibility, so that
the protocol path can be studied without the distractions of bus-interconnect adapters or
technology-specific pad cells. \Cref{fig:arch-three-layer} shows the layering.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Host/register layer.} The hand-written control and status register (CSR) file
  (\texttt{csr_registers}) exposes a simple 32-bit register interface to software, and the four
  Host-Controller-Interface (HCI) queues (\texttt{hci_queues}) buffer commands, transmit data,
  receive data, and responses. This layer turns asynchronous software activity into the steady stream
  of descriptors and data words that the protocol engine consumes.
  \item \textbf{Controller/protocol layer.} The protocol engine (\texttt{controller_active}) wraps the
  command FSM (\texttt{flow_active}), the clock generator (\texttt{scl_generator}), the byte/bit
  serialisers (\texttt{bus_tx_flow}, \texttt{bus_tx}, \texttt{bus_rx_flow}), the bus-condition monitor
  (\texttt{bus_monitor}), and the dynamic-address-assignment subsystem
  (\texttt{entdaa_controller}, \texttt{entdaa_fsm}). It is the only layer that understands the I3C and
  \iic{} frame structure.
  \item \textbf{PHY layer.} The physical-interface shim (\texttt{i3c_phy}) provides two-flip-flop
  metastability synchronisation on the incoming \texttt{scl}/\texttt{sda} lines and the open-drain (OD)
  / push-pull (PP) output drivers. It contains no pad primitives, so the design remains technology
  independent and simulatable without a foundry library.
\end{enumerate}

The guiding philosophy is deliberate simplicity: a \emph{single clock domain}, a \emph{single command
authority} (the \texttt{flow_active} FSM, which is the sole originator of bus activity for ordinary
transfers), and a clean separation between the software-visible contract and the protocol timing. This
philosophy is what makes the design tractable as a study vehicle, and it motivates most of the
reference-derived decisions discussed in \cref{sec:arch-boundary}.

\section{Top module and block diagram}
\label{sec:arch-top-block}

The synthesis top, \texttt{i3c_controller_top}, instantiates exactly four children, mirroring the three
layers: \texttt{u_csr} (\texttt{csr_registers}), \texttt{u_queues} (\texttt{hci_queues}),
\texttt{u_ctrl} (\texttt{controller_active}, which in turn instantiates the protocol children), and
\texttt{u_phy} (\texttt{i3c_phy}). \Cref{fig:arch-top-block} shows the full instance hierarchy and the
top-level port groups.

At the boundary, the top module presents three port groups. The \emph{register bus} carries
\texttt{reg_addr_i[11:0]}, \texttt{reg_wdata_i[31:0]}, \texttt{reg_wen_i}, \texttt{reg_ren_i},
\texttt{reg_rdata_o[31:0]}, and \texttt{reg_ready_o}. The \emph{bus pads} carry the synchronised inputs
\texttt{scl_i}/\texttt{sda_i} and the active-low open-drain outputs
\texttt{scl_o}/\texttt{sda_o}/\texttt{sda_oe_o} together with \texttt{sel_od_pp_o}. The remaining ports
are the system \texttt{clk_i} and the active-low asynchronous reset \texttt{rst_ni}. Internally, the
top wires the CSR to the queues on the software side (command and transmit are written by software and
read by hardware; receive and response are written by hardware and read by software), the queues to the
protocol engine on the hardware side, the shared 1-cycle-latency Device Address Table (DAT) read port
to \texttt{controller_active}, and the eighteen timing-parameter buses (nine I3C and nine \iic{} values)
from the CSR to the protocol engine.

\section{Transaction dataflow}
\label{sec:arch-dataflow}

\Cref{fig:arch-dataflow} traces the canonical write and read paths end to end, which is the most useful
way to understand how the layers cooperate.

\paragraph{Write path.} Software assembles a 64-bit command descriptor by writing
\texttt{CMD\_QUEUE\_PORT} twice: the first write stages the low DWORD, the second write supplies the
high DWORD and pushes the combined 64-bit descriptor into the command queue. For data-bearing
transfers, software also writes the payload words to \texttt{PIO\_DATA\_PORT}, which feeds the transmit
queue. The \texttt{flow_active} FSM pops the descriptor, fetches the addressed DAT entry through the
hardware read port, drives the address and data phases through \texttt{bus_tx_flow} and
\texttt{scl_generator} (multiplexed by \texttt{controller_active}) out to \texttt{i3c_phy} and the pads,
and finally writes a response descriptor into the response queue, which software reads back through
\texttt{RESP\_PORT}.

\paragraph{Read path.} The read path is identical up to the address phase; thereafter
\texttt{flow_active} enables \texttt{bus_rx_flow}, which samples \texttt{sda} on each SCL rising edge,
assembles bytes, and packs them into 32-bit words that are committed to the receive queue. Software
drains the receive queue through \texttt{PIO\_DATA\_PORT} and reads the terminating response descriptor
from \texttt{RESP\_PORT}.

The dynamic-address-assignment (ENTDAA) flow is a third path: \texttt{flow_active} issues the broadcast
header and the ENTDAA Common Command Code (CCC), then hands the bus to the ENTDAA subsystem, which runs
the per-Target arbitration loop and streams each enumerated device's identification word back through
the receive queue (\cref{sec:rtl-entdaa}).

\section{Clock, reset, and signal conventions}
\label{sec:arch-clk-reset}

The entire controller runs in a \emph{single clock domain} on \texttt{clk_i}. Because all bus timing is
counted in system-clock cycles (\cref{sec:rtl-csr}), the design needs a fast reference: the default
timing values assume a system clock of \SI{333.333}{\mega\hertz}, which is the minimum that lets the
single-counter generator resolve the SDR \SI{12.5}{\mega\hertz} push-pull period and the sub-cycle
setup and hold windows with integer counts. Reset is the active-low, asynchronously asserted
\texttt{rst_ni}; every sequential block follows the \texttt{always_ff~@(posedge~clk_i~or~negedge~rst_ni)}
idiom so that reset takes effect immediately and release is synchronous.

Two distinct reset mechanisms coexist. The hardware reset \texttt{rst_ni} returns all state to its
initial value. A separate \emph{software reset} --- \texttt{RESET\_CONTROL[0]}, a self-clearing pulse
--- synchronously flushes the four HCI queues and the CSR command/transmit staging registers without
disturbing the protocol FSM; software is expected to assert it only while the controller reports idle.

Inputs from the bus are metastability-hardened before use. \Cref{fig:arch-clk-reset} shows the chain:
\texttt{i3c_phy} provides a two-flip-flop synchroniser on each of \texttt{scl} and \texttt{sda}, and
\texttt{bus_monitor} adds one further capture flop before its edge detectors, giving three flops of
latency between a pad transition and the first combinational use of the synchronised value.

The signal-naming conventions used throughout the RTL are: the suffix \texttt{\_i} for inputs and
\texttt{\_o} for outputs; FIFO and serialiser handshakes as \texttt{*\_valid}/\texttt{*\_ready} pairs
(an action occurs only when both are asserted); active-low open-drain bus outputs
\texttt{scl_o}/\texttt{sda_o} with their output-enable \texttt{sda_oe_o}; and the phase selector
\texttt{sel_od_pp_o}, where \texttt{1} selects push-pull signalling for the data phase and \texttt{0}
selects open-drain signalling for the address and acknowledge phases.

\section{Reference-derived design boundary and decisions}
\label{sec:arch-boundary}

The design is not a clean-sheet effort but a deliberate simplification of the open-source CHIPS Alliance
\texttt{i3c-core}~\cite{chipsalliance_i3c}, a full Host-Controller-Interface implementation integrated
into a root-of-trust subsystem. \Cref{fig:arch-chips-ref} sketches the reference architecture and marks
what this thesis retains, adapts, and excludes; the quantitative size comparison is reported once, in
\cref{ch:results}.

\emph{Retained}, because they are the essence of I3C as a study target: SDR private write, read, and
Immediate Data Transfer; legacy \iic{} Fast-Mode traffic on the same two wires~\cite{nxp_i2c}; dynamic
address assignment via ENTDAA; and the ENEC/DISEC broadcast and direct CCCs. \emph{Adapted}, to keep the
software contract and the timing explicit: the auto-generated register file is replaced by a hand-written
one with a simple address/data bus instead of AXI/AHB adapters; the DAT is narrowed to 32 bits and 32
entries and the separate Device Characteristics Table is removed (software retains the Provisioned ID,
Bus Characteristics Register, and Device Characteristics Register after enumeration); a single shared
counter generates all bus timing instead of an array of parallel timers; the open-drain bus is modelled
with an output-enable convention rather than instantiated pads; and ENTDAA is re-expressed entirely from
the \emph{Controller's} perspective, completing command-issue paths that the reference leaves as
unimplemented \texttt{TODO} stubs. \emph{Excluded}, as out of scope: In-Band Interrupts, the High-Data-Rate
modes, multi-controller / secondary-controller operation, and Target mode. These cross-cutting decisions
recur in the module walkthrough of Part~B and are not repeated there.

\section{Queue, DAT, and descriptor formats}
\label{sec:arch-formats}

The software/hardware contract is carried by four queues, one address table, and a small set of
descriptors. \Cref{tab:queue-dat-overview} summarises the formats at overview resolution; the complete
bit-field layouts are tabulated in \cref{app:descriptors}.

\begin{table}[!htb]\centering
  \caption{Queue, DAT, and descriptor format overview. Full bit-field layouts appear in
    \cref{app:descriptors}.}
  \label{tab:queue-dat-overview}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.27\textwidth}
                     c c
                     >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.40\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Structure} & \textbf{Width} & \textbf{Depth} & \textbf{Role} \\
    \midrule
    Command queue        & 64-bit & 64 & Command descriptors (two software DWORD writes per entry) \\
    Transmit data buffer & 32-bit & 64 & Outgoing data words \\
    Receive data buffer  & 32-bit & 64 & Incoming data words \\
    Response queue       & 32-bit & 64 & Response descriptors (one per completed transfer) \\
    Device Address Table & 32-bit & 32 & Maps a device index to its I3C dynamic and \iic{} static address \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each command descriptor's three least-significant bits select its \emph{attribute}: a regular transfer,
an immediate data transfer (up to four in-line data bytes, no transmit-queue traffic), or an address
assignment (ENTDAA). A fourth combo-transfer encoding exists in the type package but is rejected as
unsupported by the FSM's descriptor validator. The DAT entry, shown below, is the narrowed 32-bit form:
bit~31 marks an \iic{} legacy device, bits~22:16 hold the I3C dynamic address, and bits~6:0 hold the
\iic{} static address.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog,caption={Simplified 32-bit DAT entry (\texttt{controller\_pkg.sv}).},label={lst:dat-entry}]
typedef struct packed {
  logic       device;           // [31]    1 = I2C legacy device
  logic [7:0] reserved_30_23;   // [30:23] Reserved
  logic [6:0] dynamic_address;  // [22:16] I3C dynamic address
  logic [8:0] reserved_15_7;    // [15:7]  Reserved
  logic [6:0] static_address;   // [6:0]   I2C static address
} dat_entry_t;
\end{lstlisting}

The response descriptor is a single 32-bit word: a 4-bit error-status field in the most-significant
nibble, the 4-bit transaction identifier copied from the command, and the 16-bit count of payload bytes
actually transferred. Its error field is the subject of the next section.

\section{Error-handling model}
\label{sec:arch-errors}

Errors are detected at several points in the protocol path --- a NACKed address or data byte, a transmit
underflow, a receive overflow, a short read, an unsupported descriptor, a repeatedly rejected dynamic
address, or a host-requested abort --- but they are \emph{consolidated in one place}. The
\texttt{flow_active} FSM latches each condition in a dedicated sticky flag during the transfer and, on
entering the terminal \texttt{WriteResp} state, maps the highest-priority flag to the 4-bit
\texttt{ERR\_STATUS} field of the response descriptor. The encoding follows the HCI/TCRI controller
interface convention (Table~11 of that interface), not a MIPI protocol clause; \cref{tab:err-status}
lists the full enumeration and marks the subset the controller actually generates, and
\cref{lst:err-map} shows the priority-ordered mapping function.

\begin{table}[!htb]\centering
  \caption{Response-descriptor \texttt{ERR\_STATUS} encoding. ``Gen.'' marks the codes this controller
    generates; the remaining codes are defined by the interface but not produced by the supported
    feature set.}
  \label{tab:err-status}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}c l c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.50\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Code} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Gen.} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
    \midrule
    0x0 & \texttt{Success}                    & \textbullet & Transfer completed without error \\
    0x1 & \texttt{Crc}                        &            & CRC error (HDR; not supported) \\
    0x2 & \texttt{Parity}                     &            & Parity error (not generated) \\
    0x3 & \texttt{Frame}                      &            & Framing error (not generated) \\
    0x4 & \texttt{AddrHeader}                 & \textbullet & Address-header byte NACKed \\
    0x5 & \texttt{Nack}                       & \textbullet & ENTDAA dynamic address repeatedly rejected \\
    0x6 & \texttt{Ovl}                        & \textbullet & Receive overflow or transmit underflow \\
    0x7 & \texttt{I3cShortReadErr}            & \textbullet & Target ended an I3C read early \\
    0x8 & \texttt{HcAborted}                  & \textbullet & Transfer terminated by a host abort \\
    0x9 & \texttt{I2cDataNackOrI3cBusAborted} & \textbullet & \iic{} data byte NACKed / I3C bus abort \\
    0xA & \texttt{NotSupported}               & \textbullet & Descriptor invalid or feature unsupported \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog,caption={Priority-ordered error consolidation in \texttt{flow\_active.sv}.},label={lst:err-map}]
function automatic i3c_resp_err_status_e map_resp_err_status();
  if      (addr_nack_q)      return AddrHeader;                 // 0x4
  else if (data_nack_q)      return I2cDataNackOrI3cBusAborted; // 0x9
  else if (daa_nack_error_q) return Nack;                       // 0x5
  else if (rx_overflow_q ||
           tx_underflow_q)   return Ovl;                        // 0x6
  else if (short_read_q)     return I3cShortReadErr;            // 0x7
  else if (not_supported_q)  return NotSupported;               // 0xA
  else if (hc_aborted_q)     return HcAborted;                  // 0x8
  else                       return Success;                    // 0x0
endfunction
\end{lstlisting}

Centralising error reporting in the command FSM keeps the rest of the protocol path free of
error-formatting logic: the serialisers and the clock generator simply report events, and the FSM
decides how a transfer ends.

\section{PHY (\texttt{i3c\_phy})}
\label{sec:rtl-phy}

\textbf{Role.} The PHY shim is the only module that touches the pads. On the input side it provides a
two-flip-flop synchroniser for \texttt{scl_i} and \texttt{sda_i}, both resetting to logic high to match
the idle (released) bus. On the output side it forwards the controller's driven value
\texttt{ctrl_sda_i}, its output enable \texttt{ctrl_sda_oe_i}, the clock \texttt{ctrl_scl_i}, and the
phase selector to the pad signals \texttt{sda_o}/\texttt{sda_oe_o}/\texttt{scl_o}/\texttt{sel_od_pp_o}.

\textbf{Key decision.} The PHY contains \emph{no} technology-specific pad primitives. Open-drain
behaviour is expressed purely through the output-enable convention (\cref{sec:rtl-controller-active}),
which keeps the design synthesis-target-agnostic and lets the testbench model the wired-AND bus with an
ordinary signal. The synchroniser depth (two flops here, plus one capture flop in
\texttt{bus_monitor}) is the project's metastability budget.

\section{CSR register file and Device Address Table (\texttt{csr\_registers})}
\label{sec:rtl-csr}

\textbf{Role.} The CSR module is the software-visible face of the controller: a hand-written 32-bit
register file plus the 32-entry DAT, the command/transmit staging logic, and the read/write multiplexers
for the four queue ports. \textbf{Interface.} It speaks the simple register bus of
\cref{sec:arch-top-block}; both register reads and the hardware DAT read are registered, giving one cycle
of latency. \textbf{Map.} The roughly two dozen registers fall into the groups summarised in
\cref{tab:csr-summary}; the complete bit-field map, including all eighteen timing registers and their
reset values, is given in \cref{app:csr}.

\begin{table}[!htb]\centering
  \caption{CSR register groups (overview). The full bit-field map is in \cref{app:csr}.}
  \label{tab:csr-summary}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.50\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Offset(s)} & \textbf{Group} & \textbf{Function} \\

\textbf{Key decisions.} Two are worth highlighting. First, the command port assembles a 64-bit
descriptor from two consecutive 32-bit writes: the first stages the low DWORD, the second supplies the
high DWORD and pushes the entry. Second, back-pressure is explicit and non-lossy --- when the command
or transmit FIFO is full, the CSR captures one pending write, holds its valid/data, and deasserts
\texttt{ready_o} until the FIFO accepts it, so no software write is silently dropped. Reading an empty
receive or response port returns zero without popping the FIFO. Writing the register file by hand (rather
than generating it from a register-description language, as the reference does) is the single decision
that most improves study-ability, because the entire software contract is one readable
\texttt{case} statement.

\section{HCI queues (\texttt{hci\_queues} / \texttt{sync\_fifo})}
\label{sec:rtl-fifo}

\textbf{Role.} \texttt{hci_queues} instantiates the four buffers of \cref{tab:queue-dat-overview} from a
single generic \texttt{sync_fifo} primitive: the 64-bit command queue and the three 32-bit data/response
queues, each 64 entries deep, all flushed together by the software reset. \textbf{Datapath.} The
\texttt{sync_fifo} is a classic single-clock circular buffer with one guard bit on each pointer; full and
empty are distinguished by comparing the guard bits, and the \texttt{*\_valid}/\texttt{*\_ready}
handshake gates every push and pop.

\textbf{Key decision.} The FIFO depth is constrained to a power of two by an \emph{elaboration-time}
assertion (\cref{lst:fifo-assert}) rather than by run-time logic. This turns an illegal parameterisation
into a compile-time failure --- the basis of test case FIFO\_005, which deliberately elaborates a
depth-3 FIFO and passes only if this fatal fires --- and lets the pointer arithmetic stay a simple binary
increment with no modulo.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog,caption={Power-of-two depth contract enforced at elaboration (\texttt{sync\_fifo.sv}).},label={lst:fifo-assert}]
initial begin
  assert (Depth == (1 << PtrW))
  else $fatal(1, "sync_fifo: Depth (%0d) must be a power of 2", Depth);
end
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Bus monitor (\texttt{bus\_monitor})}
\label{sec:rtl-bus-monitor}

\textbf{Role.} \texttt{bus_monitor} watches the synchronised \texttt{scl}/\texttt{sda} lines and reports
the bus conditions the protocol needs: START, Repeated START (Sr), STOP, and the per-line edge and
stability flags, all packed into a \texttt{bus_state_t}. \textbf{Datapath.} It captures the inputs once
more (the third synchroniser flop), then drives four \texttt{edge_detector} instances (SCL/SDA rising and
falling) and three \texttt{stable_high_detector} instances (SDA high, SCL high, SCL low). A START
candidate is an SDA falling edge while SCL is stably high; a STOP candidate is an SDA rising edge while
SCL is stably high; the candidate is only confirmed once the delayed edge detector agrees, which rejects
a data-bit transition during SCL-low from being mis-reported after SCL rises.

\textbf{Key decision.} START and Repeated START share the same electrical signature, so the monitor
distinguishes them with a small piece of state: once a START has been seen and not yet followed by a
STOP, any further START is reported as a Repeated START. This single bit is what lets the ENTDAA and
combined-transfer logic downstream reason about frame boundaries correctly.

\section{SCL generator (\texttt{scl\_generator})}
\label{sec:rtl-scl}

\textbf{Role.} \texttt{scl_generator} is the controller's clock and bus-condition engine. Its 14-state
FSM (\cref{fig:scl-fsm}) produces the SCL waveform during data phases and the carefully sequenced
SDA/SCL edges for START, Repeated START, STOP, and the post-STOP bus-free interval.

\textbf{Datapath --- the single-counter strategy.} Rather than instantiate a separate timer per timing
parameter, the generator keeps \emph{one} down-counter, \texttt{tcount}, and reloads it from the
appropriate CSR timing value as it walks through its states (\texttt{DriveLow}, \texttt{DriveHigh}, the
START/STOP setup and hold states, \texttt{BusFree}, and so on). The open-drain SCL-low period is selected
by \texttt{scl_use_od_low_i} so that the slower open-drain address phase and the faster push-pull data
phase use different low times from the same counter. This single-counter scheme is a deliberate area
simplification over the reference's parallel timers.

\textbf{Key decision.} The Repeated START needed to restart a frame --- for a read takeover or a direct
CCC --- is requested through one input, \texttt{gen_rstart_i}, with a companion fast-path
\texttt{takeover_i} that routes the FSM straight to \texttt{RstartSdaFall} when the command engine needs
to seize SDA for a read. The chapter describes only this implemented request/handoff path; there are no
other restart entry points.

\section{TX/RX serialisers (\texttt{bus\_tx}, \texttt{bus\_tx\_flow}, \texttt{bus\_rx\_flow})}
\label{sec:rtl-serdes}

\textbf{Role.} Three small FSMs move bytes and bits between the command engine and the bus.
\texttt{bus_tx_flow} (four states: \texttt{Idle}, \texttt{DriveByte}, \texttt{DriveBit},
\texttt{NextTaskDecision}) accepts a byte- or bit-level transmit request and walks it out one bit per SCL
period; it delegates the actual per-bit edge timing to \texttt{bus_tx} (five states: \texttt{Idle},
\texttt{AwaitClockNegedge}, \texttt{SetupData}, \texttt{TransmitData}, \texttt{HoldData}), which honours
the data setup and hold windows. \texttt{bus_rx_flow} (four states: \texttt{Idle}, \texttt{ReadByte},
\texttt{ReadBit}, \texttt{NextTaskDecision}) is the receive counterpart, sampling SDA on each SCL rising
edge. \Cref{fig:serdes-fsm} shows the three machines.

\textbf{Key decision.} The receive path aligns the acknowledge/T-Bit slot with the data it follows: the
deserialiser presents the seven already-sampled bits together with the live SDA value on the eighth cycle,
so that the assembled byte is valid exactly when \texttt{rx_done} asserts and the command engine can act
on the trailing bit in the same step. This live-SDA alignment removes a pipeline bubble that would
otherwise appear at every byte boundary.

\section{ENTDAA subsystem (\texttt{entdaa\_controller} + \texttt{entdaa\_fsm})}
\label{sec:rtl-entdaa}

\textbf{Role.} Dynamic Address Assignment is handled by a two-level subsystem, re-expressed entirely from
the Controller's perspective. The outer \texttt{entdaa_controller} (seven states: \texttt{Idle},
\texttt{StartLoop}, \texttt{RequestRStart}, \texttt{WaitRStart}, \texttt{ReadDAT}, \texttt{RunEntdaa},
\texttt{Done}) manages the enumeration loop: it reads the DAT entry for the current round, invokes the
inner FSM to enumerate one device, and advances the round until either no device answers or a STOP is
seen, at which point it reaches \texttt{Done}. The inner \texttt{entdaa_fsm} (eight states: \texttt{Idle},
\texttt{SendRsvdByte}, \texttt{ReadRsvdAck}, \texttt{ReceiveIDBit}, \texttt{SendAddr},
\texttt{ReadAddrAck}, \texttt{WaitStop}, \texttt{Done}) performs one per-Target handshake.
\Cref{fig:entdaa-fsm} shows both machines and \cref{fig:entdaa-flow} renders the loop as an algorithm.

\textbf{Per-Target handshake.} The inner FSM transmits the reserved address \texttt{7'h7E} with the
read direction bit, reads the acknowledge (a NACK means no further device is arbitrating), then shifts in
64 identification bits most-significant-first --- the 48-bit Provisioned ID, the 8-bit Bus
Characteristics Register, and the 8-bit Device Characteristics Register. It then transmits the assigned
seven-bit dynamic address together with its odd-parity bit and reads the Target's acknowledge. On
completion it presents the captured PID/BCR/DCR to the command engine, which streams them back to
software through the receive queue. A STOP detected mid-handshake forces the inner FSM directly to its
\texttt{Done} state.

\textbf{Key decision.} Placing the entire DAA protocol on the Controller side is the change that
completes what the reference left unfinished, and it is why ENTDAA appears as a first-class, verifiable
path in this design rather than as a Target-side stub. The outer/inner split keeps the loop bookkeeping
(rounds, DAT indexing, repeated-rejection detection that raises the \texttt{Nack} error of

\section{The \texttt{flow\_active} \FlowStateCount-state command FSM (flagship)}
\label{sec:rtl-flow-active}

\texttt{flow_active} is the headline RTL contribution: the single command authority that turns a popped
descriptor into a complete bus transaction and a response. It is implemented as a
\FlowStateCount-state machine (\cref{lst:flow-enum}), and unlike the reference --- which leaves several
command-issue paths as \texttt{TODO} --- every state and every supported transaction path is realised and
verified. \Cref{fig:flow-fsm} is the full-page state diagram and \cref{fig:flow-issue} renders the
command-issue decision as an algorithm; \cref{tab:flow-states} summarises the role of each state, with
the exhaustive transition table relegated to \cref{app:fsm-tables}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog,caption={The \FlowStateCount{} states of \texttt{flow\_active} (\texttt{flow\_fsm\_state\_e}).},label={lst:flow-enum}]
typedef enum logic [3:0] {
  Idle              = 4'd0,  WaitForCmd        = 4'd1,
  FetchDAT          = 4'd2,  WaitDAT           = 4'd3,
  I3CBcastHeader    = 4'd4,  IssueImmediateCcc = 4'd5,
  FetchTxData       = 4'd6,  InitI3CWrite      = 4'd7,
  InitI3CRead       = 4'd8,  InitI2CWrite      = 4'd9,
  InitI2CRead       = 4'd10, IssueCmd          = 4'd11,
  WriteResp         = 4'd12
} flow_fsm_state_e;
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{State architecture.} The machine has a short common prologue, a branch fan-out, a set of
per-transfer issue paths that converge on a shared work state, and a single epilogue. From \texttt{Idle}
it advances to \texttt{WaitForCmd} when the controller is enabled, pops a descriptor into
\texttt{FetchDAT}, and validates it: an invalid or unsupported descriptor sets the \texttt{NotSupported}
error and jumps straight to \texttt{WriteResp}, while a valid one launches the 1-cycle DAT read and waits
in \texttt{WaitDAT}.

\textbf{Branch decision out of \texttt{WaitDAT}.} This is the heart of the FSM and the subject of the
algorithm in \cref{fig:flow-issue}. Using the command attribute, the target type (I3C vs \iic{}, read
from the DAT entry), the transfer direction, and whether a broadcast header is required, the FSM selects
one of the issue paths: an immediate or address-assignment command goes through \texttt{I3CBcastHeader};
an I3C private write or read goes to \texttt{InitI3CWrite}/\texttt{InitI3CRead} (optionally preceded by
the broadcast header); and an \iic{} transfer goes to \texttt{InitI2CWrite}/\texttt{InitI2CRead}. A
continuation of an in-progress frame (a back-to-back transfer to the same I3C Target) skips the broadcast
header.

\textbf{Issue paths.} The \texttt{Init*} states drive the START (or Repeated START) and the address byte
with its acknowledge, switching \texttt{sel_od_pp} between the open-drain address phase and the push-pull
data phase. Regular writes pull payload from the transmit queue via \texttt{FetchTxData}; immediate
writes take their bytes directly from the descriptor with no queue traffic. CCCs are emitted from
\texttt{IssueImmediateCcc}, which handles both the broadcast form (CCC code plus its odd-parity T-Bit,
followed by an optional event byte for ENEC/DISEC) and the direct form (a Repeated START, the Target's
dynamic address, and the defining byte). Address assignment enters \texttt{IssueCmd} and hands the bus to
the ENTDAA subsystem of \cref{sec:rtl-entdaa}.

\textbf{Convergence and read handling.} All data movement converges on \texttt{IssueCmd}, which sequences
data bytes for writes and reads. The read sub-path is the most intricate: it commits each completed
32-bit receive word, recognises an early Target termination as a short read, performs the SDA takeover
needed to end an I3C read, and --- when chaining is possible --- can loop back to \texttt{FetchDAT} for a
supported continuation rather than ending the frame.

\textbf{Abort and epilogue.} A host abort is handled by an override at the top of the next-state logic: in
any abortable state it steers the machine toward a clean STOP and then to \texttt{WriteResp}, latching the
\texttt{HcAborted} (or, for an interrupted \iic{} read, the appropriate data-NACK) status. The single
epilogue state, \texttt{WriteResp}, formats the response descriptor through the consolidation function of
\cref{lst:err-map} and returns to \texttt{Idle} once the response queue accepts it.

\section{Integration: \texttt{controller\_active} and OD/PP switching}
\label{sec:rtl-controller-active}

\textbf{Role.} \texttt{controller_active} is the integration glue of the protocol layer. It instantiates
the command FSM, the ENTDAA subsystem, the clock generator, the serialisers, and the bus monitor, and it
arbitrates the bus between the command FSM and the ENTDAA subsystem: while ENTDAA is active, the transmit
and receive requests, the DAT read port, and the Repeated-START request are sourced from the DAA logic;
otherwise they come from \texttt{flow_active}. It also selects the active timing-parameter set, switching
every value between the I3C and \iic{} register banks according to the FSM's \texttt{use_i2c_timing}
output.

\textbf{OD/PP output formation.} The module composes the single driven \texttt{sda} from three
prioritised sources --- the clock generator (which owns SDA during START/STOP/Sr), a low-bit handoff
takeover, and otherwise the transmit serialiser --- and encodes open-drain versus push-pull entirely in
the output enable, as shown in \cref{lst:odpp} and \cref{fig:odpp-switch}. In push-pull mode
(\texttt{sel_od_pp = 1}) the driver is always enabled; in open-drain mode the driver is enabled only to
pull the line low (\texttt{\textasciitilde sda}), letting the external pull-up provide the high level.
This output-enable convention is exactly what lets the PHY omit real pad cells.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog,caption={SDA composition and output-enable open-drain modelling (\texttt{controller\_active.sv}).},label={lst:odpp}]
assign ctrl_sda_o = scl_gen_driving_sda ? scl_gen_sda :
    handoff_takeover_q ? 1'b0 : tx_flow_sda_drive ? tx_flow_sda : 1'b1;
assign ctrl_sda_oe_o = scl_gen_driving_sda ? ~scl_gen_sda
    : handoff_takeover_q ? 1'b1
    : tx_flow_sda_drive ? (tx_flow_sel_od_pp | ~tx_flow_sda) : 1'b0;
assign sel_od_pp_o = (scl_gen_driving_sda || handoff_takeover_q) ? 1'b0
    : tx_flow_sel_od_pp;
\end{lstlisting}

This completes the structural and behavioural walkthrough of the design. The cross-cutting decisions that
recur across the modules were consolidated in \cref{sec:arch-boundary}; the full register map, descriptor
layouts, port lists, and state-transition tables are collected in
\crefrange{app:csr}{app:fsm-tables}; and all quantitative metrics --- module sizes, the reduction
against the reference, and the verification results --- are reported in \cref{ch:results}.

  high level. Open-drain versus push-pull is encoded entirely in the output enable: in push-pull the
